USS Abraham Lincoln Crew Hits Thai Shore After Record 270 Days at Sea

2026-09-03 4 min read

After nearly nine months without touching land, the 5,000 crew members of the USS Abraham Lincoln have finally arrived in Pattaya, Thailand – a resort town with a reputation as infamous as their deployment was grueling.

The aircraft carrier's crew, who set a record for the longest uninterrupted time at sea, were greeted at the Hard Rock Cafe and Hotel by local officials, the city's mayor, police, and women in traditional Thai dress as they stepped off buses in civilian clothes, bags slung over their shoulders.

"I don't know how to say it other than there's a disconnect between reporting back home and what's happening on the ship," Captain Dan Keeler told reporters, responding to questions about deteriorating living conditions onboard during the marathon deployment.

'A Fever Dream' for Tired Sailors

Most crew members appeared to have been instructed not to speak with journalists about onboard conditions, but their excitement at finally hitting dry land was palpable.

"It's a fever dream right now," said one sailor, Breese, who declined to give his surname. He said he was looking forward to trying Thai food for the first time and meeting new people after months confined to the carrier.

Shakeira Fairfax, who traveled from New Jersey to reunite with her daughter Jada, expressed mixed emotions. "I just want to pick her up and spin her around. She's my baby always," Fairfax said. But she admitted, "I don't know who I'm welcoming home."

Jada completed basic training last October, was deployed a month later, and celebrated her 25th birthday onboard in June – just months after the U.S. and Israel launched their war on Iran in February. Fairfax said communication stopped abruptly when hostilities began but had since become intermittent.

Conditions and Controversy

Captain Keeler acknowledged the carrier "looks like a dirty car in a parking lot" but pushed back against reports of dire conditions. He likened first-time sailors' complaints to "sending kids off to college for the first time" and claimed the ship had been "thrown around like a football" during an election cycle.

While insisting there was plenty of food onboard, Keeler admitted fresh fruit and vegetables ran out. "I am looking forward to a fresh salad," he said. Supplies of soap and toothpaste had to be carefully rationed to ensure everyone received some.

When asked about reports of attempted suicides onboard, Keeler declined to comment, citing an ongoing investigation. Responding to President Trump's remarks that the 270-day deployment was "not nearly long enough," Keeler stated simply: "The president speaks for himself."

Ethan Johnson, a cook on the accompanying USS Higgins, took a more stoic approach when asked about conditions: "We joined the military, it's not a cruise ship."

Pattaya's Complicated Welcome

Pattaya's history with the U.S. military runs deep. Once a quiet fishing village, it was transformed during the Vietnam War into one of the world's most infamous red light districts when American troops used bases in Thailand.

"In my opinion, it's really the founding influence for the town," said Pipatpong Fakfare, a tourism specialist at Bangkok University. "The US military turned a quiet fishing village into a global destination for an international nightlife and entertainment hub in about 15 years."

Although American troops left by the mid-1970s, the sex industry infrastructure persisted, with waves of European, Chinese, and Russian tourists taking their place over the decades.

Local authorities have imposed restrictions on the sailors' shore leave. Mayor Poramet Ngampichet confirmed that Soi 6 alley and neighboring areas known for go-go bars would be off-limits after sunset.

However, a massive neon sign at the entrance to Walking Street – the town's other entertainment and red light district – welcomed the carrier's crew.

Sailors are also barred from water sports and pharmacy visits. Thai authorities want to prevent access to medications available without prescription in Thailand that would require one in the United States.

Strategic Port Call

Kristopher Robinson, a naval attaché with the U.S. embassy, said Pattaya was chosen "because the people of Thailand have a history of being wonderful hosts not just to US sailors but people from all around."

The crew will be released in staggered groups over several days, with Robinson emphasizing they would conduct themselves in accordance with both U.S. and local laws.

"Sailors here, like all tourists, they're going to enjoy Pattaya's great food and wonderful culture," he said.
