Iran Launches Retaliatory Strikes on Gulf Neighbors After US Bombardment Hits Wedding Celebration

2026-09-03 5 min read

A dramatic escalation in the six-month conflict between Iran and the United States unfolded Wednesday as Tehran launched missiles and drones at several Gulf countries and Jordan, retaliating for American airstrikes that Iranian officials say killed four civilians at a wedding party.

The Iranian military released footage showing what it claimed were missiles fired toward Jordan, where American forces maintain a significant presence. Sirens sounded in Kuwait City during the early hours as projectiles targeted multiple locations across the region, according to local reports.

Wedding Strike Sparks Outrage

The catalyst for this latest round of hostilities appears to be a Tuesday evening U.S. strike on a home in Kuhestak, a small coastal town overlooking the strategic Strait of Hormuz. Iranian state media reported that the building was hosting a wedding celebration when it was hit, killing two women and two children aged 4 and 16. At least 68 others sustained injuries.

Disturbing images from Iran's semiofficial Tasnim news agency showed wounded children receiving treatment at a hospital in nearby Minab – the same town where a U.S. missile strike on the war's opening day killed over 100 children at a primary school.

Iranian officials initially reported five deaths but later revised the number to four without explanation. The semiofficial Fars news agency released security camera footage showing fireballs illuminating the night sky over Kuhestak following multiple explosions.

"Very Precise Weapon" Under Scrutiny

Weapons experts examining fragments of the missile used in the wedding strike identified it as a U.S.-made SLAM-ER cruise missile. Trevor Ball of Armament Research Services characterized it as "a very precise weapon" that can be guided in flight by an operator using infrared camera feeds.

N.R. Jenzen-Jones, the organization's director, noted the same missile type is also used by the United Arab Emirates and Saudi Arabia – though neither country has reported conducting strikes on Iranian territory.

"There are now 108 injuries and four confirmed martyrs from the American attacks," Iran's health minister told state broadcaster IRIB, confirming the deaths of four Revolutionary Guard members and 10 Basij volunteer forces.

U.S. Central Command spokesman Capt. Tim Hawkins acknowledged being "aware of the reports" but would not confirm whether an investigation had been launched.

Economic Warfare Intensifies

The conflict, now in its seventh month, has sent shockwaves through global energy markets. Brent crude oil prices have climbed approximately 30% to around $95 per barrel since fighting resumed.

The critical Strait of Hormuz – through which roughly one-fifth of the world's traded oil typically passes – remains largely closed, with only a handful of vessels making the passage each day. Iran has regularly attacked ships attempting transit, despite repeated American pronouncements that the strait is open.

Saudi shipping company Bahri reported two Filipino sailors were killed in an attack Monday as their vessel transited the waterway.

Iran's economy continues to buckle under sanctions and a U.S. naval blockade. The rial hit another record low Wednesday, with traders exchanging 2.20 million rials for one U.S. dollar – a 10% increase from last week's already-historic low.

Regional Allies Under Fire

U.S. Gulf allies Kuwait and Bahrain confirmed they had come under attack, though no casualties were reported. In Iraq's Kurdish region, authorities said they intercepted 10 explosive-laden drones near Irbil, while Iranian television claimed American bases there had been targeted.

Israel's military reported that Iran-backed Hezbollah fired two explosive drones at soldiers in southern Lebanon, causing no injuries.

Iran's recently appointed Supreme National Security Council head Mohsen Rezaei issued a defiant warning on social media: "You will soon see that Iran's new strategy on the battlefield, in diplomacy, and in confronting the economic blockade will shatter your foundations."

Diplomatic Deadlock

The violence follows a monthlong lull in fighting, with mediation efforts by Pakistan failing to produce lasting results. A ceasefire agreement reached in June collapsed within weeks amid mutual accusations of violations.

Secretary of State Marco Rubio stated that Iran would "continue to feel the squeeze" from Washington until it abandons nuclear weapons ambitions and ends support for regional armed groups. Tehran has consistently maintained its nuclear program is peaceful.

President Trump took a hardline stance on his social media platform Tuesday night, writing: "I couldn't care less if they sign a worthless, to them, agreement. I like our position now much better, with almost total control of the Hormuz Strait, and their economy totally collapsing. They are just playing out the inevitable. When are the Iranian people going to rise up and fight?"

Dangerous Cycle

Hamidreza Azizi, senior Iran analyst at the Crisis Group, warned that neither side can sustain the current pattern indefinitely. "At some point, one of the sides may see a need to escalate in order to break this cycle. So that could bring the two sides to a point that they have been trying to avoid until now, which is a full-scale war."

The conflict has created mounting political problems for the Trump administration ahead of November's midterm elections, as American military resources are stretched increasingly thin across the region.

Sascha Bruchmann, an analyst with the International Institute for Strategic Studies in Bahrain, framed the violence as a calculated response to shifting leverage. "The U.S. is trying to reinstall some kind of confidence into the global oil markets … and the Iranians had to respond to that by reinserting insecurity and doubt," he said.
